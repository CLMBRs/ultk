Do neural networks really find monotone quantifiers easier to learn — and why?

Session 2026-07-26 Branch learn-quant-paper-reproduction Commit fcbec10 Paper: Haberland & Steinert-Threlkeld, Quantifiers that are more monotone are easier to learn (SALT 35: 286–309)

A living tracker for the follow-up research on the SALT 35 paper: what we assume, what we're asking, what we've tested, and what we found. Written to be readable without the original conversation. Source of record: src/examples/learn_quant/; this page lives at analysis/research_dashboard.html.

The story in one minute

The paper. We generated 2,000 artificial quantifiers (formulas like "all A are B", "more A than B", or stranger machine-made ones), taught each one to small neural networks by showing labeled examples, and measured how hard each was to learn. Headline result: quantifiers that are more monotone — a logical property that all simple determiners in natural languages have — were reliably easier to learn. That supports the idea that languages keep monotone quantifiers because they're easier to learn.

The puzzle this session attacks. Digging into the data after publication revealed something odd: the effect is almost entirely driven by downward-monotone quantifiers (the "no…", "at most…" family). Upward-monotone ones ("some…", "at least…") barely get any advantage. That's strange, because there's a mathematical argument (explained below) that the learner shouldn't care about direction at all.

What we found. Two experiments this session both point the same way: the direction effect is not something the network does. It comes from (a) which quantifiers our formula-generator happened to produce — the downward ones it made tend to have example sets that are statistically easy to tell apart — and (b) noise in how we measure directional monotonicity. The paper's main claim survives, but its explanation gets sharper: what makes a quantifier easy is largely how separable its true and false examples are, and monotonicity-as-sampled correlates with that.

Primer: the six terms you need

everything below uses these — skim once, refer back as needed
Quantifier expression
A machine-generated formula built from set operations, e.g. subset_eq(A, B) = "all A are B" or not(equals(cardinality(A), cardinality(B))) = "A and B differ in size". 2,000 of them were used for training.
Situation (model)
One small "scene": a handful of objects, each tagged as belonging to set A only, set B only, both, or neither. The network's input is an encoding of one scene; the label says whether the quantifier is true there.
Truth set / decision boundary
The collection of scenes where the quantifier is true. The "boundary" is the divide between true scenes and false scenes — the shape the network must internalize. Two quantifiers that are exact opposites share one boundary; they just call different sides "true".
Upward / downward monotone
Upward: truth survives enlarging a set — "some cats meow" stays true if we add more cats. Downward: truth survives shrinking it — "no cats meow" stays true if cats are removed. The paper's innovation is measuring this in degrees from 0 (not at all monotone, like "an even number of") to 1 (perfectly monotone, like "all"), in four senses: upward/downward × left/right argument.
Learning difficulty (validation-loss AUC)
The network's total accumulated prediction error across training — the area under its error curve. Low ≈ 200: learned almost immediately. High ≈ 5,000+: still making errors late in training. All "AUC" numbers below are in these units; differences of a few tens are negligible, differences of hundreds-to-thousands are what the interesting effects look like.
Complement pair
Two quantifiers in our trained sample that are exact opposites: one is true in precisely the scenes where the other is false. "all A are B" vs "not all A are B". Both members were independently trained, which is what makes the T1 experiment possible.
What "model" means in T1
No new neural model was trained for this follow-up. The LSTM and Transformer difficulty scores come from the original completed training runs. T1 newly executes each symbolic quantifier on set-theoretic scenes (called QuantifierModel objects in the code) and fits one ordinary linear regression for comparison. A QuantifierModel is a scene, not a neural network.

This session's three headline numbers

12%
How much of the "downward is easier" trend survives the strictest possible test
We found 36 trained pairs of exact-opposite quantifiers. Within such a pair the learning problem is identical except which side is labeled "true" — so if direction itself mattered to the network, the downward member should win by a predictable margin (computed from the sample-wide trend). The actual within-pair advantage was only 12% of that margin. Direction per se is nearly inert.
−80%
How much of the direction effect is explained away by what the training examples look like
Across all 1,795 trained quantifiers, the statistical advantage of downward monotonicity shrinks from β −708 to −138 (per SD) once we control for simple descriptions of each quantifier's true-vs-false example sets. Most of "downward is easier" is really "easy-to-separate examples happen to be downward" in our sample.
4×
Example-set statistics beat monotonicity as a predictor of difficulty
Those same example-set descriptions predict learning difficulty with R² = 0.54; the monotonicity degrees manage 0.13. The single best predictor: how far apart the average true scene and average false scene are ("class separation", r = −0.68 — more separation, less difficulty).
Synthesis. The upward/downward asymmetry lives in which quantifiers our grammar generated and in measurement noise — not in the learner. The one mediator that dominates (class separation) is identical for a quantifier and its opposite, which is exactly why opposites learn equally fast (T1) while the sample-wide trend is strong (T2). The two experiments tell one consistent story.

What we knew coming in

verified facts from the earlier reproduction review — the baseline all of this builds on
  • The data is trustworthy. 8,005 training runs (7,172 completed training; 2,000 unique quantifiers), and the analysis CSV matches the original experiment database byte-for-byte.
  • The paper's headline replicates. Correlation between monotonicity degree and difficulty: r = −0.333 here vs −0.3453 published (the tiny difference comes from a post-publication bug fix in the monotonicity computation; the published regression table reproduces to within 0.2%).
  • It's not just formula length. Longer formulas are harder (r ≈ +0.23), but monotonicity predicts difficulty on top of length, with ~3.4× more unique explanatory power.
  • It's not an operator artifact. The effect survives controlling for which of the 11 operators each formula uses (β −540/SD, p = 2e-38) — so it's not, say, "formulas with or are hard and also non-monotone".
  • But it's directionally lopsided (the Q2 puzzle): downward monotonicity predicts easiness strongly (β −571/SD, p = 1e-39); upward predicts nothing (+80/SD, n.s.).
  • And the degree distribution has a suspicious gap (the Q3 puzzle): almost no quantifiers score just above zero monotonicity (1.7% in (0, 0.15]) while 8.8% sit at exactly 0 — traced to how the measure behaves on noise-free formula-generated quantifiers, with equality-type predicates the only route into the gap.

The three open questions

Q1 · Does the grammar decide which monotonicity profiles exist at all?

Open — experiment designed, not yet run

Why it matters: a quantifier's monotonicity is fixed by its meaning; the formula-generating grammar only chooses which meanings get produced. If the sample's monotonicity distribution (and the downward surplus) is a fingerprint of our particular grammar, then claims like "more monotone → easier" are partly claims about the grammar, not about learning. The plan (EXPERIMENT_PLAN.md): extend the operator inventory so both polarities are expressible, randomize the grammar's production weights, generate ~60k quantifiers, and map how grammar knobs causally shift the monotonicity distribution. No neural training needed — degrees are cheap to compute. Seven predictions pre-registered.

Q2 · Why do downward quantifiers get all the learning advantage?

Major progress — two experiments done this session

Why it's genuinely puzzling: the network never sees the formula — only (scene, true/false) pairs. Its training objective treats "learn Q" and "learn the opposite of Q" as the same problem with labels swapped. And the opposite of an upward-monotone quantifier is always a downward-monotone one. So the shape of the true/false boundary can't favor a direction — any real asymmetry has to sneak in through which quantifiers got sampled, how the example scenes are drawn, how scenes are encoded, or how we measure direction. This session tested the two cheapest channels head-on (T1, T2 below).

Q3 · Why does almost nothing score "slightly monotone"?

Mechanism identified — confirmation on the real model space pending

Why it matters: a measure that can only output "zero-ish" or "0.2 and up" on realistic inputs quietly turns a continuous claim ("more monotone → easier") into a partly categorical one. The earlier review showed the gap is a joint product: the measure is bimodal on noise-free (formula-generated) quantifiers, and the grammar only produces noise-free quantifiers; equality-type predicates ("exactly as many A as B") are the lone crisp route into the gap. Remaining: replicate that simulation on the real model space, audit two known edge cases in the measure's code, and re-check under the alternative "switches" measure.

Experiment T1 · The opposite-pairs test

scripts/negation_pair_test.py → analysis/tables/11_negation_pair_test.txt

Why this test

It's the cleanest possible probe of the symmetry argument above. A quantifier and its exact opposite share one true/false boundary and differ in monotonicity direction — the opposite of "all A are B" (downward in A) is "not all A are B" (upward in A). If the learner truly finds downward quantifiers easier as such, that advantage must show up between the two members of such a pair. If instead both members learn equally fast, direction itself is inert, and the sample-wide direction effect must come from elsewhere.

Exactly what was run

No network was trained or retrained in T1. The experiment combines two kinds of evidence. First, it recomputes semantics: each grammar formula is evaluated as a deterministic Python function on 20,000 freshly sampled scenes to verify that the two formulas always return opposite truth labels. Second, it reuses learning outcomes already recorded for those formulas: validation-loss AUC from the original 3-layer, 20-hidden-unit LSTM and 2-layer Transformer (d_model 12, 4 heads) training runs. The only fitted model in this script is a small OLS regression, used to ask how large a within-pair gap the sample-wide upward/downward trend would predict.

Follow the computation: notebooks/negation_pair_test_walkthrough.ipynb shows the source CSV, architecture settings, one semantic complement check, the surviving pair table, the paired AUC differences, and the predicted-versus-observed calculation in separate cells.

What we did

Scanned all 2,000 trained quantifiers for pairs whose truth sets are exact complements: 44 pairs found. One catch: truth sets were compared on the small 256-scene universe used for the monotonicity measure, but training scenes were drawn from a much larger space — so we re-checked each pair on 20,000 freshly sampled training-style scenes. 41 of 44 survived; 3 pairs are opposites only on the small universe (a warning that the measure's universe and the training distribution genuinely differ). 36 of the 41 have completed training runs for both members; those are the test set.

First define the pair labels and "strong contrast"

Every expression has two summary scores between 0 and 1: downward degree says how close it is to perfectly downward-monotone, and upward degree says how close it is to perfectly upward-monotone. We summarize its directional leaning as downward degree − upward degree. This ranges from +1 (completely downward, not upward) to −1 (completely upward, not downward).

Inside each complement pair, we call the member with the larger directional leaning D (more downward-dominant) and the other member U. These are relative labels: in a weak pair, neither member need be a clean textbook downward or upward quantifier.

Strong-contrast pair, defined: take D's directional leaning and subtract U's directional leaning. We call the pair "strong contrast" when that difference is greater than 0.20 on the 0–1 degree scale: (downD − upD) − (downU − upU) > 0.20. This is an operational sensitivity cutoff, not a theorem or a learned threshold. It removes pairs whose two members receive nearly the same directional scores, because those pairs cannot strongly test a direction-based learning prediction. Eleven of the 36 analyzable pairs pass it.

How the predicted-versus-observed comparison is built

  1. Learn the sample-wide rule of thumb. Using all 1,795 expressions with complete outcomes, fit a linear regression that predicts mean validation-loss AUC from downward and upward degree. A regression coefficient is simply the estimated AUC change associated with changing one score while holding the other fixed. Because the two scores have different spreads, they are first expressed in standard deviations (SDs): one SD is a typical-sized difference in that score. The fitted rule says +1 SD downward predicts 708 less AUC (easier), while +1 SD upward predicts only 20 more AUC (essentially no sample-wide effect).
  2. Turn that rule into a prediction for each pair. For a given pair, calculate how many SDs U differs from D in downward degree and in upward degree. Multiply those two differences by −708 and +20, respectively, then add them. The result is the predicted gap: how much harder U should be than D if the sample-wide direction rule still applies after holding the semantic boundary fixed.
  3. Compare prediction with what the networks actually did. The observed gap is much simpler: mean AUC(U) − mean AUC(D), using the already-recorded LSTM and Transformer outcomes. We average the predicted gaps across the 11 strong-contrast pairs, separately average their observed gaps, and divide: 65.5 observed ÷ 558.4 predicted = 0.117 ≈ 0.12. This is a ratio of two difficulty gaps—not accuracy, variance explained, or a p-value.

One pair, with the arithmetic exposed

Consider "No A are B" and its complement "Some A are B". D ("No") scores down = 1.000, up = 0.000, so its leaning is +1.000. U ("Some") scores down = 0.123, up = 1.000, so its leaning is −0.877. Their contrast is 1.000 − (−0.877) = 1.877, easily above 0.20.

Verdict. Opposite quantifiers — same boundary, flipped direction — are learned essentially equally fast. The mathematical symmetry argument holds in practice, and at most ~12% of the sample-wide direction effect can be a within-quantifier phenomenon. The other ~88% must come from which quantifiers the grammar sampled or from the measurement itself. That redirects the whole Q2 investigation.

Bonus finding: the direction measure itself is noisy

For exact opposites there's a definitional sanity check: "how upward is Q" should equal "how downward is its opposite" — they describe the same fact about the same boundary. In the data this identity fails for 37 of 44 pairs: worst error 0.79 on a 0–1 scale. So some of the "upward doesn't matter" result may simply be that upward degrees are poorly measured. This is the main motivation for the metric audit below (next-steps #1).

Metric audit · Root cause and fix for the direction measure

notebooks/monotonicity_metric_mirror_audit.ipynb

What was wrong

The entropy-based monotonicity measure (upward_monotonicity_entropy in measures.py) scores how well a single predictor variable — "does this model have any true predecessor in the subset order?" — predicts the quantifier's truth value. For upward degree that predictor is perfectly natural: a true predecessor means "going up in the order makes more things true." For downward degree, the code used a successor-based predictor instead: "does this model have any true model above it?" These two predictor families are not mirrors of each other in any algebraic sense, so the identity down(Q) = up(¬Q) can fail — and does, badly, for 37 of 44 complement pairs (max error 0.788).

Why it fails

Imagine a chain of three models a ⊆ b ⊆ c and a quantifier Q = (0, 1, 0). The upward predictor of ¬Q asks: "does each model have a false predecessor?" — the answer is yes for every model (a itself is false), so the predictor is a constant, giving score 0. The original downward predictor of Q asks: "does each model have a true successor?" — the answer is yes for a and b (b is true), no for c, giving a non-constant predictor and a positive score. The predictor designs look toward opposite ends of the order, so they measure genuinely different things.

The fix — two lines in measure_monotonicity

The semantic insight is that "Q is downward-monotone" and "¬Q is upward-monotone" are exactly the same statement: monotonicity direction only depends on which side of the truth table you call True. So the downward score should call the upward function with the negated quantifier — not a flipped direction parameter. Changing

# OLD: flip=True changes the direction of the partial-order search
measure(all_models, ref_A, quantifier, cfg, flip=True)   # right downward

# NEW: negate labels, keep the predecessor-looking direction
measure(all_models, ref_A, 1 - quantifier, cfg)          # right downward

guarantees down(Q) = up(¬Q) and up(Q) = down(¬Q) by construction, because both calls go through the same code path. Verified in the audit notebook: 44/44 pairs now pass all four mirror identities with max error 0.00 (down from 37/44 failing at max error 0.788 before the fix).

See the full proof: notebooks/monotonicity_metric_mirror_audit.ipynb — cells 1–6 prove the failure; cell 7 (the new section) verifies the fix. The actual code change is in measures.py lines 172–181.

Experiment T2 · What actually predicts difficulty?

scripts/truth_set_stats.py → analysis/tables/12_truth_set_stats.txt

Why this test

If the learner doesn't care about direction (T1), why is "downward is easier" so strong across the sample? Suspicion: downward-monotone quantifiers, as our grammar happened to generate them, tend to have training examples that are statistically easy to tell apart — and it's the tell-apart-ability doing the work, with direction along for the ride. That's testable without any new training: describe every quantifier's example sets with a few plain statistics, and see whether the direction effect survives once those statistics are held fixed.

What we did

For each of the 2,000 quantifiers, we sampled 4,000 scenes the same way training data was made and computed, separately for its true scenes and false scenes: how full the sets A and B are on average, how much that varies, how often the quantifier is true, and — the key one — class separation: the distance between the "average true scene" and the "average false scene" profiles. Intuition: if true scenes typically look like "3 things in A, none in B" and false scenes like "1 in A, 4 in B", a learner can score well with crude statistics from day one; if the two profiles coincide, every distinction is hard-won. Then we re-ran the difficulty regressions with and without these statistics as controls.

Verdict. Most of the direction effect is explained by what the examples look like, chiefly class separation. Crucially, class separation is the same for a quantifier and its opposite (the two class profiles just swap roles) — so this account predicts precisely what T1 observed: no gap within opposite pairs, big gradient across the sample. One mechanism, both results.

What the conclusions rest on

assumptions and limits — read before trusting the verdicts above

Facts about the original pipeline we rely on

  • The network only ever sees (scene, label) pairs — never the formula. Its loss function (sigmoid + binary cross-entropy) is exactly symmetric under swapping the labels, which is what makes the T1 symmetry argument valid.
  • Training scenes were drawn with balanced true/false classes from scenes of 16 objects (12 eligible for the quantifier's sets) — we mimic this exactly when re-sampling.
  • The monotonicity degrees were computed on a small 256-scene universe, a different space from the training scenes. T1's three failed pairs prove this mismatch has real consequences.
  • "Difficulty" = accumulated validation error (AUC), averaged over each quantifier's completed runs per architecture.

Choices made this session (and their limits)

  • "Exact opposite" was verified on 20,000 sampled scenes — overwhelming evidence but not a logical proof. (The check has teeth: it rejected 3 of 44 candidates.)
  • Example-set statistics use 4,000 scenes per quantifier (fixed seed) and describe scenes by zone counts — a summary of the input, not the literal network encoding.
  • Regressions are ordinary least squares on per-quantifier averages, matching the earlier review sections; p-values are descriptive, not preregistered-confirmatory.
  • 36 pairs is a small sample: T1 can rule out the large predicted effect (+558 vs observed +66 ± 94) but cannot distinguish a zero effect from a small one — hence the "≤ 12%" phrasing, not "zero".
